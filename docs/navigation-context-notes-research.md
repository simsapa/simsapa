# Navigation context notes — research record

The evidence behind each entry in `assets/navigation-context-notes.toml`, so a
note can be reviewed and refined without redoing the search. How the notes are
stored and shown is in
[sutta-navigation-tree.md §2f](./sutta-navigation-tree.md).

## 1. What a note states

Every note answers the same four questions, in this order:

1. **Time period** — `Origin: <century or range>`, first, so it survives the
   row's two-line truncation.
2. **Country** — where the text was composed, as far as it is known.
3. **Probable author** — the traditional ascription *and* whether scholars
   accept it. "Compilers unknown" is an answer; a blank is not.
4. **How well anchored the date is** — one of:

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Well anchored** | An external, datable fact fixes it: a reign, a dated translation, an inscription. |
| **Partly anchored** | An external fact fixes one end or one layer; the rest is estimated. |
| **Estimated** | Scholars broadly agree, but only on internal and comparative evidence. |
| **Contested** | Named scholars give materially different dates, or the ascription is disputed. |
| **Uncertain** | No real evidence either way. |

## 2. Limits of this research

Read these before trusting any single note.

- **The sources are secondary and mostly tertiary.** Nearly everything below is
  a Wikipedia article summarising von Hinüber's *Handbook of Pāli Literature*,
  Norman's *Pāli Literature*, Warder, Cousins or Frauwallner. None of those
  books was read directly. Where a note names a scholar, it is quoting the
  summary, not the scholar.
- **"Northern India" for the early canon is an inference, not a cited finding.**
  It rests on the First Council being placed at Rājagaha in Magadha and on the
  argument that the texts' political geography matches the Buddha's era. No
  source consulted states a place of composition for a specific collection.
- **"Probably India" for the late Khuddaka books has no source at all.** Nothing
  consulted says where the Vimānavatthu, Petavatthu, Apadāna, Buddhavaṁsa or
  Cariyāpiṭaka were composed. It is the default assumption for canonical Pāli
  texts other than the Parivāra, and is marked "probably" for that reason.
- **The Buddha's own dates are contested**, which moves every "5th century BCE"
  in the notes. Gombrich places his death between 422 and 399 BCE, Bechert at
  368 BCE, and Japanese scholars at about 400, 386 or 368 BCE. The notes
  therefore write "5th–4th century BCE" or "5th–3rd century BCE" rather
  than "during the Buddha's lifetime".
- **King Mahānāma's reign is given differently by different sources.** The
  notes use "c. 410–434"; Wikipedia gives 412–434, and the Samantapāsādikā
  article gives 384–430 CE by converting a Buddhist-era date (927–973 BE). The
  384–430 figure was not used.

## 3. The four Nikāyas

**Keys:** `dn`, `mn`, `sn`, `an`

| Claim in the note | Evidence |
|---|---|
| 5th–3rd century BCE; largely fixed before Aśoka | The canonical texts are silent about Aśoka, the basic reason earlier scholars held them complete before him. Aśoka's edicts name specific texts, so some were fixed by his reign. |
| Written down in Sri Lanka, 1st century BCE | The Mahāvaṁsa account: written down under King Vaṭṭagāmaṇi at the Alu Vihāra, "no earlier than 29–17 BC". |
| By tradition recited by Ānanda at the First Council | The council account: Ānanda recited the suttas, Upāli the Vinaya, at Rājagaha. Scholars doubt the council's historicity. |
| Contested | Gombrich, Sujato & Brahmali and Wynne hold that much goes back to the Buddha's time; Schopen holds there is no definite evidence of the canon's contents before the 5th–6th century CE. The earliest Pāli manuscript fragments are from the Pyu cities, mid-5th to mid-6th century CE (Wynne cites late 3rd–early 4th). |

**Not sourced:** "gathered by length / by topic / by number" is the ordinary
description of how each Nikāya is arranged, and "northern India" is the
inference described in §2.

**To refine:** the four notes are identical apart from the arrangement. If a
source distinguishes their ages (the Dīgha is sometimes argued to be the latest
of the four), each could say so.

## 4. Khuddaka Nikāya

The early/late division used throughout this section is Oliver Abeynayake's, as
reported on the Khuddaka Nikāya page: **early** — Suttanipāta, Itivuttaka,
Dhammapada, Thera- and Therīgāthā, Udāna, Jātaka; **late** — Khuddakapāṭha,
Vimānavatthu, Petavatthu, Niddesa, Paṭisambhidāmagga, Apadāna, Buddhavaṁsa,
Cariyāpiṭaka.

- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuddaka_Nikāya>

### Khuddakapāṭha — `kp`

- Late stratum (Abeynayake). It "had not attained canonical status until
  relatively late" and was left out of the canonical lists of the Dīgha and
  Majjhima reciters.
- "May have originated as a handbook for novices composed from excerpts from
  the canon"; all but one of its texts are found elsewhere in the canon.
- **No date and no place were found.** The note says so rather than guessing.
  An earlier draft gave "perhaps 2nd–1st century BCE"; that was unsupported and
  was removed.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuddakapāṭha>

### Dhammapada — `dhp`

- The collection is traced to the 3rd century BCE; the oldest manuscripts are
  from about 1500 CE.
- Brough identified 330–340 verses common to the Pāli, Gāndhārī and Udānavarga
  versions, pointing to a common ancestor, while rejecting the idea that any one
  version is the original.
- **To refine:** "by about the 3rd century BCE" is a single source's figure.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammapada>

### Udāna — `ud`

- "One of the earlier Buddhist scriptures"; the Pāli discourses including the
  Udāna were "largely fixed in [their] current form ... by the first century
  B.C.E."
- Von Hinüber: the Udāna was the original core of the Sanskrit Udānavarga.
- **Not sourced:** no century is given for its composition; "5th–3rd century
  BCE" is the early-stratum range.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udāna>

### Itivuttaka — `iti`

- Traditionally ascribed to Khujjuttarā, a laywoman of Kosambī, who memorised
  the Buddha's discourses and repeated them.
- "Some scholars consider it one of the earliest of all Buddhist scriptures,
  while others consider it somewhat later." Japanese scholars place it and the
  Udāna just after the Suttanipāta as the earliest texts.
- The Chinese parallel (Xuanzang, 650 CE) has 65 sūtras against the Pāli 112.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itivuttaka>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pāli_Canon>

### Suttanipāta — `snp`

- The Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga are the oldest material; Nakamura holds
  they "already existed during the lifetime of Gotama Buddha". The Khaggavisāṇa
  Sutta has a parallel among the oldest Gandhāran manuscripts.
- The canonical Niddesa comments on those chapters; Aśoka's Bairāṭ edict names
  texts from the collection.
- Ṭhānissaro: "nothing known to date allows us to conclude with certainty"
  about their primitive origin — the basis of the "contested" marker.
- No date was found for the compilation of the five-chapter collection.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutta_Nipata>

### Vimānavatthu, Petavatthu — `vv`, `pv`

- Vimānavatthu: "a relatively late addition to the Pali Canon"; some stories
  "may be earlier", with parallels in the Mahāvastu and a Sarvāstivāda
  Vimānāvadāna.
- Petavatthu: "dates to about 300 BC at the earliest".
- **Weak.** The 300 BCE floor is stated for the Petavatthu only and applied to
  both. No upper bound and no place were found.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vimānavatthu>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petavatthu>

### Theragāthā, Therīgāthā — `thag`, `thig`

- Theragāthā: most of the monks lived in the Buddha's time, "but the collection
  seems to have continued to grow until at least the Third Buddhist Council";
  chapters were added over about 300 years. Norman: early and late metres, with
  miracle stories marking later additions.
- Therīgāthā: "composed over centuries, with some dating to the late third
  century BCE". Faure: "there is no evidence that these verses were actually
  composed by the women they are attributed to."
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theragatha>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therīgāthā>

### Apadāna, Buddhavaṁsa, Cariyāpiṭaka — `tha-ap`, `thi-ap`, `bv`, `cp`

- "Considered by most scholars to have been written during the 1st and 2nd
  century BCE, and ... therefore a late addition to the Pāli Canon" (stated on
  the Buddhavaṁsa page for all three).
- Apadāna: "thought to be one of the latest additions to the canon".
  Cariyāpiṭaka: "considered to be post-Asokan".
- **Not sourced:** the place, and "told in their own voice" / "presented as
  spoken by the Buddha", which describe the texts' own framing.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhavaṃsa>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apadāna>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cariyāpiṭaka>

### Jātaka — `ja`

- Sarah Shaw: the verses are "amongst the very earliest part of the Pali
  tradition and date from the fifth century BCE", while later parts were
  incorporated up to the 3rd century CE.
- The Jātakatthavaṇṇanā (verses with prose commentary) was collected around
  500 CE.
- Bhārhut and Sāñcī reliefs with inscriptions, late 2nd–1st century BCE — the
  external anchor.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jataka_tales>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Buddhist_texts>

### Niddesa — `mnd`, `cnd`

- Traditionally ascribed to Sāriputta.
- Lévi (1925): 2nd century CE. Norman (1983): the time of Aśoka. Von Hinüber:
  it "does not belong to the old canonical texts and ... a date after Aśoka does
  not seem likely". Upper bound: "not later than the 1st century BC", the fixing
  of the canon.
- **To refine:** von Hinüber's sentence is ambiguous as summarised — it may mean
  a date *much* after Aśoka is unlikely. Check the *Handbook* directly.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niddesa>

### Paṭisambhidāmagga — `ps`

- Traditionally ascribed to Sāriputta.
- Warder: some form around the 3rd century BCE. Cousins: 1st century BCE.
  Von Hinüber: around the 2nd century CE. Norman: the term *paṭisambhidā* is
  absent from the older sutta and vinaya texts.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paṭisambhidāmagga>

### Netti, Peṭakopadesa — `ne`, `pe`

- Netti: the āryā metre was in use around 150 BCE and the text's structure
  became popular in the first centuries CE; northern India, possibly Ujjain.
  Ascribed to Mahākaccāna in the colophon, which scholars do not take literally.
- Norman: the Netti is "a rewritten version" of the Peṭakopadesa, which is why
  Dhammapāla commented on the Netti alone.
- Peṭakopadesa: **no date found at all.** The note gives only its priority to
  the Netti.
- Both are canonical in the Burmese tradition and appear in the Fifth and Sixth
  Council editions.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nettipakaraṇa>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petakopadesa>

### Milindapañha — `mil`

- "Dates from sometime between 100 BC and 200 AD." Menander I ruled in the
  2nd century BCE.
- Von Hinüber: the work is composite, "with additions made over some time" —
  the Chinese versions are much shorter — and its original language may have
  been Gāndhārī.
- **Not sourced:** "north-west India" is inferred from the Gāndhārī suggestion
  and from Menander's kingdom.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milinda_Panha>

## 5. Vinaya

### Pātimokkha — `pli-tv-bu-pm`, `pli-tv-bi-pm`

- Prātimokṣa codes survive in the Theravāda, Mahāsāṁghika, Mahīśāsaka,
  Dharmaguptaka, Sarvāstivāda and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas.
- "Most scholars consider most of the Vinaya to be fairly early, that is, dating
  from before the separation of schools."
- **Weak.** No source consulted gives a century for the Pātimokkha, and none
  says outright that its core predates the schisms — that follows from the two
  findings above. "5th–4th century BCE" is the Buddha's lifetime under the
  contested dating in §2.
- **Not sourced:** nothing was found specifically on the nuns' code.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratimokṣa>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinaya_Piṭaka>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Buddhist_texts>

### Suttavibhaṅga — `pli-tv-bu-vb`, `pli-tv-bi-vb`

- Its structure: each rule "is preceded by a story telling how the Buddha came
  to lay it down, and followed by explanations", sometimes with further stories
  as precedents.
- **No dating was found.** That the explanatory layers are later than the rules
  is the standard reading of that structure, not a cited finding, and the
  "5th–3rd century BCE" range is the span between the rules and the fixing of
  the canon.
- **Not confirmed:** that the Vibhaṅga "reached canonical form at the Third
  Council under Aśoka". The note does not say it.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suttavibhaṅga>

### Khandhaka — `pli-tv-kd`

- Tradition: compiled at the First Council, the account of each council added
  at the next.
- Frauwallner (1956): the original version was compiled at the Second Council —
  "still taken seriously by many scholars". He put at least 150 years between
  the Buddha's death and its composition.
- The Skandhakas of the different schools agree in the number and topics of
  their chapters, which is why a common origin is inferred.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khandhaka>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinaya>
- <https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/why-the-mahasa-ghika-is-not-the-earliest-vinaya/7458>
- <https://ia802901.us.archive.org/33/items/253EarlyVinayaAndTheBeginningsOfBuddhistLiteratureErichFrauwallnerSeePhilosophyOfBuddhism/253%20Early-Vinaya-and-the-Beginnings-of-Buddhist-Literature%20Erich-Frauwallner%20%28See%20Philosophy%20of%20Buddhism%29.pdf>
  (Frauwallner's book — found, not read)

### Parivāra — `pli-tv-pvr`

- "Includes a long list of teachers in Ceylon", so it is recognised as late in
  its present form; some suggest it is later than the Fourth Council in the last
  century BCE.
- The colophon names a monk Dīpa, "probably of Sri Lanka", as the author (DPPN).
  The commentaries nonetheless treat a sixteen-part Parivāra as part of the
  Vinaya rehearsed at the First Council, which the DPPN reconciles by supposing
  later enlargement.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parivāra>
- <https://aimwell.org/DPPN/parivara.html>
- <https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/introduction-to-the-parivara/36825>

## 6. Aṭṭhakathā

### Samantapāsādikā — `cst:atthakatha/vinayapiṭaka-aṭṭhakathā`

- Composed at Anurādhapura by Buddhaghosa, at the request of an elder, "during
  the reign of King Siripala", from the Sinhala
  Mahā-aṭṭhakathā with reference to the Mahāpaccarī and Kurundī.
- Translated into Chinese by Saṅghabhadra in **489 CE** — the anchor.
- Authorship: the Aṭṭhakathā page says only the Visuddhimagga and the four
  Nikāya commentaries are accepted as his by consensus; Heim argues for a wider
  corpus that includes the Samantapāsādikā. Hence "ascribed to".
- **Not sourced:** the identification of King Siripāla with Mahānāma.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samantapasadika>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhaghosa>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atthakatha>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahanama_of_Anuradhapura>

### The four Nikāya commentaries

**Keys:** `cst:atthakatha/suttapiṭaka-aṭṭhakathā/dīgha-nikāya-aṭṭhakathā`,
`…/majjhimanikāya-aṭṭhakathā`, `…/saṁyuttanikāya-aṭṭhakathā`,
`…/aṅguttaranikāya-aṭṭhakathā`

- Sumaṅgalavilāsinī, Papañcasūdanī, Sāratthappakāsinī, Manorathapūraṇī: "Only
  the Visuddhimagga and the commentaries on the first four nikayas are accepted
  by a consensus of scholars as Buddhaghosa's."
- Buddhaghosa lived about 370–450 CE and arrived in Sri Lanka in the reign of
  Mahānāma.
- His biography rests on the prologues to his works, the Cūḷavaṁsa (13th
  century) and the Buddhaghosuppatti, the last regarded as legend.
- The older Sinhala commentaries are said to have been written down with the
  canon in the last century BCE; no date for their compilation was found.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atthakatha>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhaghosa>
- <https://buddhistuniversity.net/content/articles/building-the-theravada-commentaries_hinuber-oskar>
  (von Hinüber on Buddhaghosa and Dhammapāla as authors — found, not read)

### Khuddaka commentaries — `cst:atthakatha/suttapiṭaka-aṭṭhakathā/khuddakanikāya-aṭṭhakathā`

One note on the group, because the books inside have different authors.

- Anonymous: Dhammapada-aṭṭhakathā, Jātaka-aṭṭhakathā, the Apadāna commentary.
  The Dhammapada commentary is "attributed to Buddhaghosa" and dated to the 4th
  or 5th century CE.
- Dhammapāla: seven Khuddaka commentaries (the Paramatthadīpanī) — Udāna,
  Itivuttaka, Vimānavatthu, Petavatthu, Thera- and Therīgāthā. Born in
  Kāñcipuram, lived near modern Chennai, followed the Anurādhapura tradition.
  His date "is uncertain, but he wrote after Buddhaghosa and probably no later
  than the 7th century".
- Buddhadatta: the Buddhavaṁsa commentary. Upasena: the Niddesa commentary.
  Mahānāma: the Paṭisambhidāmagga commentary.
- Ascription of the Khuddaka and Abhidhamma commentaries to Buddhaghosa "has
  been rightly questioned by modern research".
- **To refine:** the node holds 24 books and this one note names nine. A note
  per book would be more useful than a group note; the Jātaka commentary alone
  spans seven nodes.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atthakatha>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammapāla>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammapada>
- <https://accesstoinsight.org/noncanon/fieldguide>

## 7. Ṭīkā

### Sāratthadīpanī — `cst:vin01t1.tik`, `cst:vin01t2.tik`, `cst:vin02t.tik`

The same note on all three volumes.

- Composed by Sāriputta of Sri Lanka in the reign of Parakkamabāhu I
  (1153–1186).
- Parakkamabāhu's 12th-century reform unified the monastic fraternities under
  the Mahāvihāra and put fresh emphasis on the Vinaya, producing new summaries
  and commentaries (Crosby).
- Sub-commentaries in general "were begun during the reign of Parākramabāhu I"
  under Sāriputta, Mahākassapa of Dimbulāgala and Moggallāna.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samantapasadika>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sāriputta_(12th_century)>
- <https://buddhistuniversity.net/content/articles/sariputtas-three-works-on-the-samantapasadika_crosby-kate>
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcommentaries,_Theravada>

### Vajirabuddhi-ṭīkā — `cst:vin06t.nrf`

- The oldest known sub-commentary on the Vinaya; ascribed to Vajirabuddhi, by
  the DPPN to "Mahāvajirabuddhi Thera of Ceylon".
- Undated: after the 7th and before the 12th century, between the Anurādhapura
  and Polonnaruva periods, with the 9th century argued as a terminus post quem.
- One study finds "strong evidence" that it originated in a tradition connected
  with South India, "if not in South India itself" — hence "Sri Lanka or South
  India".
- <https://ours.ou.ac.lk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ID-91_TEXTUAL-TRANSMISSION-OF-THE-VAJIRABUDDHIṬIKA-A-COMPARATIVE-ANALYSIS-OF-NIDANAVAṆṆANA-SINHALESE-AND-BURMESE-EDITIONS.pdf>
- <https://aimwell.org/DPPN/v.html>
- <https://buddhism.lib.ntu.edu.tw/search/search_detail.jsp?seq=540723>

### Nikāya sub-commentaries

**Keys:** `cst:tika/suttapiṭaka-ṭīkā/dīghanikāya-ṭīkā`,
`…/majjhimanikāya-ṭīkā`, `…/saṁyuttanikāya-ṭīkā`, `…/aṅguttaranikāya-ṭīkā`

- Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṁyutta: the Līnatthappakāsinī, the "old" ṭīkā, by a writer
  "probably also called Dhammapāla". Scholars do not accept the 12th-century
  tradition identifying him with the Khuddaka commentator.
- "In the second half of the first millennium most of the literary activity in
  Pali seems to take place in the Tamil country, associated with such names as
  Ānanda and Dhammapāla" — the basis for the period and the country.
- Aṅguttara: four ṭīkās are ascribed to Dhammapāla (Līnatthappakāsinī I–IV) and
  four to Sāriputta (Sāratthamañjūsā I–IV). The CST text is the latter: the
  closing verses of `s0404t.tik.xml` name the work *Sāratthamañjūsā* and name
  Parakkamabāhu. The Dīgha files likewise name themselves *Līnatthappakāsanā*,
  and `s0104t.nrf.xml` names the *Sādhuvilāsinī*.
- Sādhuvilāsinī (the Sīlakkhandhavagga-abhinavaṭīkā, `cst:s0104t.nrf` and
  `cst:s0105t.nrf`): by Ñāṇābhivaṁsa, later 18th century, one record giving
  1801; he lived at Amarapura, the city founded by King Bodawpaya. Mentioned in
  the Dīgha group's note because those two volumes sit inside it.
- <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammapāla>
- <https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/jpts/article/view/27986>
- <https://palitextsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/03-Cousins-JPTS-34-2021.pdf>

## 8. Not covered

No note has been written, and no research done, for:

- the **Abhidhamma** — the Piṭaka, its commentaries and its sub-commentaries.
  One finding is on file: Western scholarship puts the origin of the Abhidhamma
  Piṭaka around the 3rd century BCE, and holds that Moggaliputta Tissa only
  began the Kathāvatthu at the Third Council, with debates added later.
  (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidhamma_Piṭaka>,
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathāvatthu>)
- the other **Vinaya ṭīkās** (Vimativinodanī, Vinayālaṅkāra, Vinayavinicchaya
  and the rest);
- the **Añña** collection (Visuddhimagga, the chronicles, the grammars, the
  Ledi Sayadaw works);
- the **Sutta and Vinaya roots** and the **Khuddakanikāya** group, deliberately:
  their contents span several periods, and each child carries its own note.

## 9. Where to look next

The single most useful step is to check the notes against the two standard
handbooks, which every source above is summarising:

- Oskar von Hinüber, *A Handbook of Pāli Literature* (1996) —
  <https://buddhistuniversity.net/content/reference/handbook-pali-literature_hinuber>
- K. R. Norman, *Pāli Literature* (1983).

The notes most in need of it are marked **Weak** or **Not sourced** above:
the Pātimokkha and Suttavibhaṅga dates, the Vimānavatthu and Petavatthu, the
Khuddakapāṭha, the Peṭakopadesa, and every "probably India".
